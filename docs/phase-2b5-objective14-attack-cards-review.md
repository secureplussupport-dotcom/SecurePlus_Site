## Primary-source qualifications

[NIST hash-function properties](https://csrc.nist.gov/projects/hash-functions) separate finding any colliding pair, an input for a given digest and another input matching a specified message. The latter two are preimage and second-preimage problems. Neither matching digest nor collision vocabulary proves safe content or source authentication.

[RFC 4270 §§2,4](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4270.html) explains generic classical collision effort at roughly **2^(n/2)** for an ideal n-bit output and distinguishes the specified-input problem. Its signature-substitution discussion depends on who selects the messages and what the protocol/victim accepts. This historical document is used for those distinctions, not its outdated SHA-1 feasibility statements or current algorithm recommendations.

[The original SHAttered paper §§1–2](https://shattered.io/static/shattered.pdf) describes generic birthday search separately from its special SHA-1 cryptanalytic construction. Its colliding PDFs use deliberately constructed structure and prefix conditions. That result does not prove that a birthday search cheaply replaces any pre-existing contract, reverses a digest or breaks every hash/signature. The card's contract example stays qualified; no collision, exploit or signature operation was executed here.

[RFC 8446 §4.1.3 and Appendix E.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8446.html) describes negotiated-parameter protection and version-downgrade checks, with protocol/mode limits. Attacker influence, accepted weaker choices and missing/ineffective defenses must be established before declaring a successful downgrade. An ordinary older-peer negotiation does not itself prove attack or decryption. The TLS-to-SSL card example is an illustrative name cue, not verified current browser behavior or an unconditional modern-encryption break.
