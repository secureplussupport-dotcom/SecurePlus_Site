## Primary-source semantic qualifications

[RFC 4302 §§1,3](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4302.html) separates AH integrity/data-origin authentication from encryption, excludes mutable IP-header fields and makes effective replay resistance dependent on receiver checking. Source-authentication vocabulary does not prove unique-person attribution, protected plaintext, security-association setup or network deployment.

[NIST SP 800-56A Rev.3](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/56/a/r3/final) distinguishes finite-field and elliptic-curve key establishment. [RFC 5246 Appendix F.1.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5246.html) distinguishes anonymous versus authenticated agreement and recommends fresh DH private values for DHE's forward-secrecy property. These are prerequisites beyond the short card names; they do not make agreement a signature or application-data cipher.

[RFC 8446 §§2.2,4.2.9 and Appendix E.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8446.html) ties forward secrecy to exchange mode and secret erasure: (EC)DHE can support it; PSK-only psk_ke does not satisfy that stated property. Early data has different guarantees. PSKs may be external or derived from an earlier session. PFS is a session property, with no promise for exposed session secrets, endpoint plaintext or future sessions after long-term-key theft. Existing question/PBQ qualifications are preserved without universalizing a cipher-suite name or rewriting the source.

[FIPS 186-5](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/fips/186-5/final) describes digital-signature purposes; [RFC 6979 §§1–4](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6979.html) describes ECDSA and secure deterministic per-signature-value generation while retaining private-key generation/custody requirements. ECDSA remains a signature scheme rather than encryption or ECDHE. Signature validity must be distinguished from key trust and statement truth. No cryptographic operation, device or algorithm implementation was tested.

[RFC 7468 §§2,10–11,14](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7468.html) describes labeled text encodings of cryptographic structures, including distinct unencrypted and encrypted private-key forms, and warns about untrusted/ambiguous inputs. Base64/text delimiters confer neither key protection nor certificate trust. The card's general certificates-and-keys wording is broader than its certificate-only glossary collision; the plain card does not render a repairing glossary overlay.

PKCS requires a numbered member/version and task: [RFC 8017](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8017.html) covers PKCS #1 RSA schemes, [RFC 5958](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5958.html) updates PKCS #8 private-key syntax, [RFC 2986](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2986.html) defines PKCS #10 certification requests and [RFC 7292](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7292.html) defines PKCS #12 personal-identity exchange. The bare family name establishes none of their operations, universal key contents, trusted certificates or secure export; family terminology is not one file format or algorithm.

[RFC 9257](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9257.html) discusses external-PSK provisioning, identity binding, sufficient entropy and risks from sharing a PSK among multiple endpoints. The card omits those custody/protocol premises. Sharing a secret does not identify one holder uniquely, prove secure provisioning or establish forward secrecy. PSK authentication is not automatically a strong password-based exchange. No secret, provisioning operation, authentication or compromise-response action was executed.
